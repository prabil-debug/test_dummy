# Could Two People Really Rebuild Humanity? (working title)

Status: DRAFT 1, awaiting approval. Facts below must be verified against sources before recording.
Style: dry, deadpan, "Luckily / Unluckily" device, one everyday analogy, callback ending.

## Script

**Hook**
Imagine everyone on Earth disappears, except two people. They get the whole planet, every empty house, every full supermarket. And one job: bring humanity back. It sounds romantic. It's actually a maths problem, and the maths is brutal.

**The rules**
Let's set the rules. One man, one woman. Both healthy, both thirty, and strangers to each other. No cloning, no tech support, and nobody left to ask for help.

**Wall one: the numbers**
Step one: have children. Step two: they have children. Step three: repeat until there are eight billion of you.
On paper, that's quick. If the population doubled every generation, about every twenty-five years, you'd need roughly thirty-three doublings. That's around eight hundred years.
But doubling means every couple raises four children who all survive and all have four of their own. Real humanity took until about the year 1800 to reach its first billion, and it had a head start of a few hundred thousand years.

Luckily, the maths on paper is fast. Unluckily, paper doesn't catch the flu.

**Wall two: the genes**
Here's the part nobody wants to talk about. When the first children grow up, who do they marry?
Their brothers and sisters. And every one of us carries a handful of harmful gene variants that are normally hidden, because the other parent's copy covers for them. Relatives share the same hidden variants. So their children are far more likely to inherit two bad copies. Studies of children born to close relatives find much higher rates of serious illness and early death.
Conservation biologists have a rule of thumb for saving a species: around fifty animals to survive the short term, and around five hundred to stay healthy for the long run. For humans, many researchers would want thousands.
Two isn't on that list. Two isn't even the cover charge.

**Wall three: the knowledge**
Say the genes work out. Now the survivors need a dentist. A doctor. An antibiotic. A hundred and fifty years ago, a scratch from a rusty nail could kill you, and our two survivors would have twenty-first-century bodies and none of the twenty-first-century help.
In 2009, a designer named Thomas Thwaites tried to build a toaster from scratch: mining the iron, making the plastic himself. It took nine months, and the toaster barely worked.
A toaster. Now try antibiotics.

**The analogy**
Here's the best way to see it. Think of humanity as a library, not a population. Nobody holds the whole library. There's a famous essay called "I, Pencil" that points out that no single person on Earth knows how to make a pencil. The wood, the graphite, the rubber, the paint, the metal: thousands of people, none of whom planned it.
Eight billion people aren't just eight billion mouths. They're eight billion pages of how to do things. Two people are two pages.

**The twist**
So could they actually do it? Biologically, maybe. We know humans can come back from small numbers. Some genetic studies suggest our own ancestors once shrank to a few thousand breeding individuals, though scientists still argue about the exact number.
Thousands is a recovery. Two is a coin flip with very long odds.

**The ending**
So the best advice for our two survivors is simple: find more people. And if two can't restart humanity, that tells you what humanity actually is.
Not one clever species, but a crowd that worked it out together. The thing keeping eight billion people alive is eight billion people.
Luckily, our survivors would have the whole planet. Unluckily, they'd have to share one toaster.
