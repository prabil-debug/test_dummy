# What If India Had Only 200 Million People?
Working title. Landscape 1920x1080, 2D stick-figure scenes, ~6 min. Gradual scenario, balanced tone.
Status: DRAFT 1 for approval. Lines marked VERIFY need a source check before recording (see FACTS.md).

## THE QUESTION
Right now, almost one in every six people on Earth lives in India.
That's about 1.46 billion people.
Now imagine only one in forty did.
Same country. Same rivers. Same borders. Just 200 million people instead.
Would life be calmer? Would everyone be richer? Would the trains finally be empty?
The answer is more surprising than you'd think.
Let's find out.

## THE RULES
First, the rules.
Nobody disappears. Nothing terrible happens.
Instead, India's population simply stopped growing, long ago, at around 200 million.
For comparison, India had about 238 million people back in 1901.
Everything else stays the same: the land, the rivers, the monsoon, the borders.
So what changes?

## SPACE
Start with space.
Today, India has about 490 people for every square kilometre.
At 200 million, that drops to about 67.
That's roughly the same as Mexico today.
Now think about an ordinary morning.
Mumbai packs in more than 25,000 people per square kilometre. Packed trains. Packed buses. Packed classrooms.
In a 200 million India, the same morning looks different.
A seat on the train. A shorter queue at the school gate. A flat with a spare room.
Cities would likely be smaller, with more space between the buildings.
But there's a catch.
Fewer people also means fewer taxpayers to build and run the railways, the roads and the schools, across the same enormous country.
Luckily, there's more room. Unluckily, there's less money to fill it.

## WATER, LAND AND AIR
Now, the things you can't make more of: water, land and food.
Official figures put India at roughly 1,500 cubic metres of water per person each year. Below 1,700 counts as water stressed.
With about seven times fewer people sharing the same rivers and groundwater, each person would have about seven times as much.
More farmland per person. Less pressure on forests. Fewer vehicles and factories.
But pollution isn't only about how many people there are. It's about how they live.
A smaller, richer India might drive more cars and use more electricity per person.
Fewer people don't automatically mean cleaner air. They just change the maths.

## FARMS AND FOOD
Farms are a good place to see it.
In 1970, the average farm in India was about 2.3 hectares.
By 2015, it had shrunk to about 1.08.
Why? Land gets divided as families grow. Today, around 86 percent of Indian farms are smaller than two hectares.
In a country whose population stopped growing, farms would be split far less often.
Bigger farms. More machines per field. Maybe higher earnings for each farmer.
Maybe. A bigger farm isn't automatically a better one.
But a farmer with more land and fewer people to share it with starts from a stronger place.

## THE MONEY QUESTION
Now, the money question.
India's economy is worth about 4.3 trillion dollars a year.
Divide that by 1.46 billion people, and you get roughly 2,800 dollars each.
Now divide the same economy by 200 million.
That's about 21,500 dollars each. More than seven times richer.
Sounds perfect, right?
Here's the trap.
The economy wouldn't stay the same size.
An economy isn't a pie that gets sliced thinner. It's made by people. Workers. Customers. Engineers. Farmers. Shop owners.
Take most of them away, and you don't just have fewer mouths. You have far fewer hands, and far fewer buyers.
India's median age is about 29. In the United States and China, it's about 38.
That's an enormous young workforce.
A 200 million India would have a smaller market, a smaller workforce, and less weight in the world.
So would people be richer? Maybe.
It depends on whether the economy shrinks faster than the population.
History has both: small countries that are very rich, and small countries that are very poor.
Size alone doesn't decide it.

## JOBS
Now the part that worries economists most: jobs.
Every year, millions of young Indians reach working age and look for work.
Estimates of how many new jobs India needs each year range from about 8 million to over 20 million, depending on who's counting.
Creating that many jobs, year after year, is one of the hardest problems the country faces.
In a 200 million India, far fewer young people would arrive each year.
Less competition for each job. Wages might rise.
But there's a catch.
Fewer people also means fewer customers to hire for, fewer new businesses, and fewer ideas.
Jobs aren't a fixed number. They're created by demand, and demand comes from people.

## A SMALLER GIANT
There's a way to check this.
Countries of around 200 million people already exist.
Brazil, Pakistan and Nigeria each have somewhere between 200 and 250 million.
A 200 million India would still be one of the biggest countries on Earth.
That's what most people miss.
It wouldn't be a small country.
It would just be a smaller giant.

## THE SLOWDOWN
Here's something that surprises people.
India's population is already slowing down.
According to UN estimates, the average Indian woman now has fewer than the 2.1 children needed to keep a population steady.
Growth continues for now, because so many people are young and still starting families.
But the UN expects India to reach about 1.7 billion, and then begin to fall.
So the real question isn't whether India's population will stop growing.
It's when, how fast, and what the country looks like when it does.

## FIFTY YEARS LATER
Now fast-forward fifty years.
A country that stops growing early also ages early.
Fewer young people supporting more older people, sooner.
So the real India faces the pressure of more people.
A 200 million India would face the pressure of fewer.
Neither is easy.
They're just different problems.

## TWO MORNINGS
Picture two children walking to school in the same city.
In today's India, she squeezes onto a crowded bus and sits in a full classroom.
In a 200 million India, she probably gets a seat, and a smaller class.
But her school might be further away, because fewer children means fewer schools.
And her parents might earn less, because the economy around them is smaller.
Same child. Same city. Different trade-offs.
Nobody wins everything.

## THE REAL ANSWER
So, would India be better off with 200 million people?
Not automatically.
More space, more water, shorter queues. Yes.
But also fewer workers, a smaller market, and a smaller voice in the world.
A big population is a strain.
It's also a strength.
The real lesson isn't about a number.
It's about what a country does with the people it has.
Right now, one in six humans lives in India.
Would you want one in forty?
