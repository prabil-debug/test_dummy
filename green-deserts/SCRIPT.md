# What If All Deserts on Earth Turned Completely Green?

## THE GREEN SAHARA
As recently as six thousand years ago, the Sahara was green.
Grass. Lakes. Rivers. Herds of animals, where today there is only sand.
It wasn't magic. Slow wobbles in Earth's orbit shifted the monsoon, and the rains moved north.
Then, around five thousand years ago, the rains left. Some studies say it happened fast.
Now imagine they came back. [beat]
Not just to the Sahara. To every desert on Earth. All of them, covered in forest.
What would that do to the planet?
Let's find out.

## THE RULES
First, the rules.
Drylands cover about forty percent of the land outside Antarctica. The driest of them, the true deserts, cover roughly a sixth.
In our story, every hot desert turns green with real forest, slowly, over decades.
The polar deserts stay as they are. Ice stays ice.
And we won't explain where the rain comes from. It simply arrives.
The interesting question is what happens next.
Start with the one thing every forest needs. Water.

## THE WATER
A large tree can pull up to three hundred litres of water out of the ground in a single day, and release it into the air.
Now multiply that by billions of trees.
In the Amazon, some research suggests about half the rain comes from the trees themselves. The forest waters itself.
That's the good news. Once a forest is established, it makes its own weather.
The bad news is the beginning. Young trees in dry ground die easily.
That's why many real attempts to green a desert have hit the same wall.
And there's a second wall, right under the trees' feet.

## THE SOIL
Forests need soil, and desert soil is thin, dry and poor.
Soil forms painfully slowly. Depending on the climate, one inch of fertile topsoil can take hundreds of years to form, and in some places up to a thousand.
So in our story, the forests wouldn't grow in a day. They would have to build their own ground first.
Fallen leaves. Roots. Fungi. Insects. Soil is something a forest slowly makes for itself.
It's a slow start. But once it gets going, it feeds itself.
And once the forest starts breathing out water, the sky begins to change.

## THE FLYING RIVERS
Forests don't only drink. They send water up into the air.
Winds carry that vapour for hundreds, even thousands, of kilometres. Scientists call these invisible streams flying rivers.
A green Sahara could shift the winds and the monsoon rains across Africa, and beyond.
Some regions might get wetter. Others, drier. Nobody can say for sure.
But there's one effect we can already estimate. And it lands on the other side of the Atlantic.

## THE DUST
Every year, wind lifts dust from the Sahara and carries it across the ocean.
About twenty-eight million tonnes of it fall on the Amazon.
Inside that dust is phosphorus, a nutrient the rainforest soil badly needs. NASA satellite data suggest around twenty-two thousand tonnes arrive each year.
Much of it comes from one place, the Bodélé Depression, the bed of an ancient lake.
These numbers are estimates. Some years bring five times more than others, and not all the phosphorus is usable by plants.
But the idea holds. The Amazon is partly fed by the Sahara.
Green the Sahara, and that dust would likely slow or stop.
A forest that grows in one desert could starve a forest on the other side of the world. [beat]

## THE HEAT
Forests are dark. Sand is bright.
Desert sand reflects roughly thirty to forty percent of sunlight back into space. A forest reflects only about eight to fifteen percent.
Swap sand for forest, and the ground absorbs more sunlight.
Trees also cool the air by releasing water vapour, a little like sweating.
Which effect wins? It depends on where, and scientists argue about it.
What's clear is that you can't simply paint the planet green and expect it to cool.

## THE AIR
Of course, trees also pull carbon dioxide out of the air.
New forests across a sixth of the world's land could lock away a great deal of carbon, if the trees survive and keep growing.
But carbon stored in a forest can return to the air if it burns or dies.
And forests in dry places burn.
So the climate maths comes with a long list of ifs.

## THE SOLAR WAY
Is there a shortcut? One surprising answer comes from a computer model.
In twenty eighteen, scientists simulated covering a huge part of the Sahara with wind and solar farms.
Dark panels absorb more sunlight than pale sand, and that warms the ground. Turbines stir the air. In the model, together they pushed the rainfall up.
Across the Sahara, rain more than doubled. In the Sahel, the dry belt to the south, it rose by roughly two hundred to five hundred millimetres a year.
More rain meant more plants, and more plants meant even more rain. A loop that feeds itself.
It's a model, not a measurement, and the scale was enormous, about nine million square kilometres.
But it shows something remarkable. Deserts and rain can talk to each other.

## THE WINNERS AND THE LOSERS
Then there are the people.
Deserts are hot and dry, but the lands around them aren't empty. About two point three billion people live in drylands, which include the semi-arid regions bordering deserts. That's roughly three in ten of all people on Earth.
Greener land could bring more water, more grazing and more farms.
But for nomadic herding peoples, whose way of life depends on open, dry land, a forest isn't automatically a gift.
Some would gain. Some would lose.
And so would the animals.
Deserts aren't empty. Plants that store water for years. Insects, reptiles and mammals built for heat and very little rain.
They would lose their home.
A desert isn't a wasteland. It's an ecosystem.
So has anyone actually tried to green one?

## THE GREAT GREEN WALL
Africa has.
The Great Green Wall aims to restore one hundred million hectares across the Sahel by twenty thirty.
It isn't really a wall. It's a patchwork of projects: trees, grasses and better farming, along a belt thousands of kilometres long.
How far along is it? The official count in twenty twenty put it at about four percent. Counting the wider region, about eighteen percent.
To hit the target, the countries involved would need to restore about eight million hectares every single year.
It's slow. But it has turned some dry land into land that can feed people.
China tried something even bigger.

## THE TREE BELT
In nineteen seventy-eight, China began a huge tree-planting programme across its dry north, and it is still running.
It helped hold some sand in place. It also ran into limits.
Critics say many of the planted trees died, and that too many were the same species.
And the trees needed water. In some places, groundwater fell by as much as nineteen metres.
More recently, some areas have tried solar panels with plants growing beneath them. Whether that works in the long run is still being tested.
The lesson is simple. Green only lasts where the water does.

## THE REAL TWIST
So here's the strangest part.
Nature has already run this experiment.
The Sahara turned green on its own, pushed by a wobble in Earth's orbit, and then it turned back.
That wasn't people. It was physics.
Which means a green desert isn't only a dream. It's a state the planet has been in before.
And a state it can leave.

## THE REAL ANSWER
So what if every desert turned green?
The Amazon might lose its dust. The winds and the rains would shift. The planet could store more carbon, and also absorb more sunlight. And the animals and people of the desert would lose their home.
Maybe the lesson isn't how to remove deserts.
It's that every landscape is a balance, and every balance has a price.
Six thousand years ago, the Sahara was green.
The rains left. [beat]
Would you bring them back?
