# What If You Could See Everyone's Body Count?

## THE NUMBER
Imagine waking up tomorrow, and every person you see has a number floating above their head.
Not their age. Not their salary.
Their body count. The number of people they've been intimate with.
Your date. Your boss. Your best friend. The stranger on the train.
Everyone can see everyone's. Nobody can hide it. [beat]
What would the world do with that number?
Let's find out.

## THE RULES
First, the rules.
The number is exact. It counts everyone, however you'd define it, and it never goes down.
It's visible to all, all the time.
So, what's the first thing people would do?
They'd look.
And then, in total silence, they'd look at their own.
But before anyone can trust the number, there's a problem.

## WHO DECIDES WHAT COUNTS
Researchers who study this point out that people don't agree on what counts.
Ask a hundred people what they'd include, and you get a hundred slightly different answers.
So in our story, the number is simply fixed, by magic, and nobody gets to argue.
In real life, every survey asks the question, and every person quietly answers a different one.
That means the numbers we have are already shakier than they look.
And that leads to the first surprise.

## THE SURPRISE
In surveys, men tend to report more partners than women, even though, on average, the numbers should match.
Researchers think some people exaggerate, and some people hide.
In a small study from two thousand three, volunteers were hooked up to a fake lie detector before answering. Under that pressure, the gap between men's and women's answers almost disappeared.
It was only a small study, but it hints at something.
With a visible number, nobody could round up, and nobody could round down.
Honesty by force.
But the real shock isn't who was lying. It's what the average turns out to be.


## THE AVERAGE
Most people assume everyone else has a much bigger number than they do.
In big U.S. surveys, the typical number, the median, has sat at around three to four across generations.
A handful.
The average looks higher, because a small group with very large numbers pulls it up. Statisticians call that skew.
And surveys often stop counting at fifty.
So when the numbers appeared, most people would feel relief. Almost everyone is closer to the middle than they thought.
Relief doesn't last long, though.
Because the number was never really about numbers. It was about judgment.

## THE SCARLET NUMBER
Researchers talk about the sexual double standard: the same number can earn one person a high-five, and another person a label.
Studies disagree about how strong it still is today.
But in a world with visible numbers, gossip would become a scoreboard.
We already know what happens when a number is public.
In twenty nineteen, Instagram tested hiding like counts, starting in Canada, because people kept comparing themselves to everyone else.
Later, it made hiding them optional. Some people found it helpful. Some still wanted to see.
A number, once visible, becomes a competition.
Even when nobody entered one.

## THE NUMBERS WE ALREADY HAVE
We already live with numbers that try to summarise people.
Grades. Follower counts. Credit scores.
A credit score is a single number that quietly shapes where you can rent and what you can borrow.
At least it mostly stays private.
A number about your private life would work the same way, only far more personal, and far easier to misuse.
And the first place it would show up is dating.

## THE DATING APP
Picture the first date in this world.
Before hello, you've already read their number, and they've read yours.
Some people would use it as a filter. Under three, over ten, never mind.
Some would feel judged before they even spoke.
And nobody would get to say anything about it. That's the cruel part.
Because a number has no story.
It doesn't say whether someone was in love, or lonely, or young, or brave.
It doesn't say whether they were kind.
And it definitely doesn't say whether they were faithful.
So how useful would it really be?

## WORK AND FAMILY
Now it gets worse. The number wouldn't stay in the dating world.
It would walk into the office. Employers aren't supposed to judge people on their private lives, but a number you can see is a number you can't unsee.
It would walk into the family. Awkward dinners. Quiet looks across the table.
Different families, and different cultures, would react in very different ways. Some would shrug. Some would never recover.
Friendships would shift. Some would deepen, because people finally had something honest to talk about.
Some would end.
A single number, rewriting every relationship at once.
But hold on. If the number tells us so little, why do we want it so badly?

## THE HEALTH QUESTION
There's one thing people might hope a number could tell them. Health.
But doctors and health agencies say that risk depends on protection and testing, not only on how many people someone has been with.
Someone with a high number who tests regularly can be safer than someone with a low number who never does.
So the number would give people a feeling of information, without the information that matters.
And there's something about numbers that's even harder to talk about.

## WHAT A NUMBER CAN'T SAY
A number can't say which of those were choices.
For some people, the number includes things that were never their choice.
Imagine that number hanging over your head, in public, forever.
That's why some things are private. Not because they're shameful.
Because the person who lived them should be the one who tells the story.
So, does any society allow people to know about a partner's past?

## THE RIGHT TO ASK
Actually, one does, in a very specific way.
In England and Wales, since twenty fourteen, people can ask the police about a partner's history of violence or abuse. It's called Clare's Law.
Notice what society chose to make knowable.
Not how many partners. Not a score.
Safety.
That's a very different kind of number. One that protects people, instead of ranking them.

## AFTER THE SHOCK
So what happens a year after the numbers appear?
Maybe, like most things we see every day, we'd stop looking.
Maybe people would learn to ask better questions: Who were you before? What did you learn? What do you want now?
Maybe the number would fade into the background, like a follower count.
Or maybe it would never stop mattering, and some people would never forgive it.
Nobody can say for sure.
But one thing is clear.

## THE REAL ANSWER
A number is a terrible summary of a person.
It can't tell you whether someone is honest, kind, careful, or ready.
Only time can. And conversation.
In a world where we could see everyone's number, the smartest people would be the ones who stopped looking at it.
Imagine a number floating above your head. [beat]
What would you want people to ask you instead?
