## THE FIFTEEN DAYS
In March twenty twenty, the United States launched a campaign with a very specific name.
Fifteen Days to Slow the Spread.
Work from home if you can. Skip gatherings of ten or more. Fifteen days, and it would be over.
Within two weeks, it was extended to forty-five.
Now imagine it never ended. [beat]
Not the virus. The emergency. The rules, the empty offices, the screens. All of it, staying on.
What would the world look like today?
Let's find out.

## THE RULES
First, an important difference.
This isn't about a virus that stays as deadly as it was. It's about an emergency that never switches off.
People still learn. People still adapt. But nobody ever says, "It's over."
And to be fair, an emergency isn't declared lightly.
The World Health Organization estimates the pandemic was linked to about fifteen million deaths in twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one, counting those it caused indirectly. The official count of deaths from Covid itself was about five and a half million.
That is why the world took it so seriously.
The harder question is how long a world can live like that.

## A CITY THAT TRIED
Melbourne gives us a clue.
Across six separate lockdowns, the city spent two hundred and sixty-two days under restrictions. At the time, that was the most of any city in the world.
Shops shut. Streets emptied. Neighbours became faces on a screen.
When it finally lifted in October twenty twenty-one, people celebrated.
But that was months, with an end in sight.
What happens when there's no end in sight?

## THE COUNTRY THAT STAYED CLOSED
Some places went much longer.
China locked down Wuhan in January twenty twenty, and kept strict versions of those rules for almost three years.
In spring twenty twenty-two, Shanghai locked down twenty-five million people for about two months.
Then, in December twenty twenty-two, the rules were dropped. Reports linked the change to protests and to a more contagious variant.
Three years is a long time for a world on pause.
It shows two things. An emergency can last far longer than anyone plans. And ending one may be harder than starting one.
Either way, the first thing to change would be work.

## THE END OF THE COMMUTE
Before the pandemic, only about five to seven percent of paid workdays in the United States were done from home.
Then, almost overnight, kitchens became offices.
Today, even with the emergency long over, about one paid workday in four is still done at home.
So in a world where the emergency never ended, that number wouldn't stay a quarter. It could become most of it.
Cities built around commuting would have to become something else. Rush hour, gone. Lunch spots downtown, empty.
Some people would save hours every day. Others would never meet their colleagues in person.
It's freedom and loneliness in the same package.
And it wasn't only adults who stayed home.

## THE EMPTY CLASSROOM
When schools closed, children moved to screens.
In the United States, the twenty twenty-two Nation's Report Card showed the biggest drop in maths scores ever recorded. Eighth graders lost eight points. By the usual rule of thumb, that's close to a year of learning.
Researchers warn that closures weren't the only cause. But the pattern was hard to miss.
In a world with no end, a whole generation grows up learning through a window.
Some children thrive that way. Many don't.
And what about the people who weren't sick with Covid at all?

## THE QUEUE FOR CARE
Hospitals filled up with one disease, and everything else had to wait.
Early in the pandemic, researchers estimated that tens of millions of operations around the world were cancelled or postponed in just twelve weeks.
Check-ups were skipped. Screenings were missed. Many people stayed away from hospitals out of fear.
Postponed care doesn't disappear. It builds up like a queue.
In a world with no end, the queue only grows.

## THE DOCTOR ON THE SCREEN
But care found a workaround.
In February twenty twenty, about one in a thousand primary-care visits for Medicare patients happened by video or phone.
By April, it was more than four in ten.
Across the whole of twenty twenty, Medicare telehealth visits rose about sixty-three times over.
A patient in a remote village could see a specialist without a four-hour drive. A busy parent could talk to a doctor from the sofa.
In a world with no end, that becomes the new normal. In the real world, some of it stayed.
Which brings us to the sky.

## THE EMPTY SKY
In twenty twenty, the number of air passengers fell by about sixty percent, according to the United Nations' aviation agency.
Planes sat parked. Borders closed. Weddings, funerals and reunions moved to screens.
In April twenty twenty, the International Monetary Fund projected that the world economy would shrink by about three percent that year, the worst since the Great Depression.
A short emergency is survivable for businesses that depend on people moving. A permanent one is not.
Airlines. Restaurants. Small shops. Tourist towns. All of them would need to become different businesses.
But the biggest cost may be one you can't see on a balance sheet.

## THE QUIET COST
In its first year, the pandemic raised anxiety and depression worldwide by about twenty-five percent, according to the World Health Organization.
Young people and women were hit hardest.
Humans are social animals. Remove the handshake, the shared table and the crowded room, and something wears down.
Yet some people found things they hadn't expected. Time with family. Quiet. Flexibility.
Both are true. That's what makes this so hard.
So how did the real world get out?

## THE WAY OUT
Faster than almost anyone expected.
The virus's genetic code was published in January twenty twenty. On the second of December that year, less than eleven months later, the first vaccine was authorised, in the United Kingdom.
Then came more vaccines, new treatments, and, slowly, immunity built up from vaccination and from infection.
In May twenty twenty-three, the World Health Organization declared the global emergency over.
But listen carefully to what it said.

## THE REAL TWIST
The emergency ended. The virus didn't.
The World Health Organization stressed that Covid-nineteen was still a global health threat, still circulating, still changing. It simply no longer needed emergency mode.
So in a way, Covid never ended.
We just stopped treating it as an emergency.
Some things never went back, either. About one paid workday in four is still done at home. Schools learned to teach online.
The question isn't whether we can live alongside it.
We already do.

## THE REAL ANSWER
So what if the emergency had never ended?
Offices would be museums. Classrooms would be windows. Hospital queues would be longer, and the sky would be quieter.
But the real world landed somewhere in between.
Some would say we kept the best parts: flexibility, science that moves fast, and an alarm that can be switched on.
And we let go of the rest.
In March twenty twenty, we were asked to stay home for fifteen days.
The emergency ended. [beat]
Which parts of it would you keep?
